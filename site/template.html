zram and zswap for MySQL: letting the InnoDB buffer pool grow past RAM

Can Linux compressed memory (zram, zswap) make an I/O-bound InnoDB workload faster by letting innodb_buffer_pool_size exceed what fits in RAM? Yes, by a lot, but only when the data compresses well and the frequently used data does not fit in RAM uncompressed.

What we found, across all rounds

  • Compressed memory wins big only when the hot data does not fit in RAM but does fit once compressed: with ~3× compressible data, zram lz4 gave +159% throughput and −70% p95 latency (round 3, repeats within 0.6%). When the hot data already fits (AuctionMark, JOB on IMDb) or the data barely compresses (HammerDB, sysbench, YCSB: 1.1–1.4×), it changes little.
  • Use zram with a lower-priority disk swap behind it, sized to the RAM you can give it: it matched zram alone while the data fit its budget and slowed down gracefully beyond it, where zram alone stalled the whole host for 20–30 minutes instead of OOM-killing. With the same compressed budget it beat zswap by 43–50% (round 5).
  • zram beat zswap and lz4 beat zstd on this server: zswap keeps writing cold pages to disk; zstd's better ratio costs CPU that becomes the limit.
  • Other ways to compress (round 4, round 6): MyRocks lz4 reached ~85% of zram (1,940–1,963 tps); InnoDB table compression +65% over no compression (1,386–1,461 tps, zlib level made no difference); ZFS lost in every configuration; InnoDB page compression saves disk, not cache.
  • Benchmark data matters: most benchmark data compresses far less than real data might; we patched sysbench (sysbench-compressible) to control it.
  • Watch the disk, not just the setting: one series ran while the datadir SSD was ~5× slower on writes and understated InnoDB compression by ~2× until repeated (round 4 correction).

The headline test

sysbench oltp_read_write, 64 threads, uniform access over 36 GB of tables (49 GB on disk), binary log off. The data was made compressible (~3× with lz4, ~6× with zstd) with sysbench-compressible: it cannot fit in the buffer pool uncompressed on this 31 GB server, but fits in RAM once compressed. 15 min warm-up + 10 min measured; every buffer pool was full of data during the measured window.

  • zram with lz4 is the clear winner: at a 40 GB pool all data stays in memory (InnoDB disk reads ~0/s), part of it compressed at ~3.3×. The server turns from I/O-bound into CPU-bound, and CPU per transaction is no higher than with disk I/O.
  • zswap trails zram (+93% vs +159%), and it is not the pool cap: raising max_pool_percent from 50 to 80 changed nothing at 40 GB (1,711 vs 1,698 tps). zswap keeps writing cold pages back to the swap disk (1–5k pages/s, the zswap shrinker is on by default in kernel 6.8), and every miss is a disk read; zram never touches the disk.
  • lz4 beats zstd once lz4 already fits the data: zstd compresses ~2× better but costs ~6× more CPU to decompress, and CPU becomes the limit. zstd is also less stable: its two runs at 40 GB differ by 11% (1,761 vs 1,563 tps), while every other repeated point is within 1%.
  • A plain swap file on a second SSD also helps (+40% at 40 GB): a fast swap device acts as an extra cache tier. Compression adds the rest.
  • Disk swap past the best size hurts: normal swap at 28 GB is slower than at 26 GB. Compressed swap made oversizing harmless in every round.

Why most benchmarks can't show this: page compressibility

The result depends on how well InnoDB pages compress. Each benchmark's data was loaded at small scale and every 4 KiB piece of its table files compressed on its own, the way zram and zswap do. Most OLTP benchmarks generate random strings, which lz4 barely compresses. Even real data sets reach only ~1.7×.

With 16K records on 4K sectors, a compressed record is stored in whole sectors, so lz4 reaches only 1.93× on this data (zram: 3.3×): the data does not fit in the ARC and reads stay on disk. zstd reaches 3.4–3.7× and serves 92–95% of the buffer pool's misses from the ARC, but runs out of CPU (56–61% kernel CPU): every ARC hit decompresses a 16K record again. ZFS itself also costs: with a normal 24 GB pool and a small 4 GB ARC it ran at 318 tps where ext4 ran at 794.
  • MyRocks with lz4 comes closest to zram: 1,940 tps with a 16 GB block cache, 1,963 in the round 6 repeat (85% of zram's 2,286, same p95). Its data is 12 GB on disk (InnoDB: ~49 GB) and stays entirely in the Linux page cache, so there are no disk reads. A larger block cache hurts (24 GB: 1,442 tps, 19.5k reads/s) because uncompressed cache crowds the compressed files out of the page cache. zstd (6.7 GB on disk) is ~20% slower and CPU-bound. MyRocks uses more CPU per transaction than InnoDB (50–65% vs 42% per 1k tps), and it is a different engine, not a drop-in setting.
  • InnoDB page compression (lz4) saves disk (21 GB instead of ~49 GB) but not cache: the buffer pool holds uncompressed pages, so it stays read-bound. Its single run (328 tps) fell in the slow-SSD period (‡) and was not repeated, so its throughput is not reliable.

Growing the data past what fits compressed (round 5)

The same compressible sysbench data, freshly loaded at 36, 48, 60 and 74 GB of tables (16 × 11–23M rows), uniform access, 64 threads, 20 min warm-up + 10 min measured. The compressed modes and the swap file use a buffer pool of 1.1 × the data size. zram + swap = a 32 GB zram device (≈10 GB of RAM at ~3.2×) at priority 100 with the 2nd-SSD swap file at priority 10; zswap + swap = max_pool_percent=32 (≈10 GB), the same budget. zram alone has a 128 GB device: no budget. Numbers are not directly comparable with round 3 (fresh loads, longer warm-up).

  • While the data fits in the zram budget, zram + swap matches zram alone (36 GB: 2,225 vs 2,175 tps) without touching the disk, and beats zswap with the same budget by ~50%.
  • Past the budget, zram + swap degrades instead of failing, but it sends the overflow to disk even while RAM is free: its fixed ~10 GB budget was too small for this server, so zram alone (no budget) stayed ahead up to 60 GB. Size zram's disksize to the RAM you can actually give it (≈ RAM left after the pool's resident part × ratio).
  • zram alone did not OOM: it took the server down. At 74 GB (88 GB swapped, ~29 GB of RAM in zram) the run finished (594 tps), but stopping MySQL afterwards left the host unresponsive to SSH for 20–30 minutes; the kernel never killed anything. Without a fallback, an oversized pool on zram can stall the whole host.
  • With the same compressed budget, zram + swap beat zswap + swap at 36–60 GB (+49%, +43%, +50%). At 74 GB both fell below normal swap with a 26 GB pool, and neither had settled after the warm-up.
  • Once most data is on disk, a normal-size pool wins again: at 60–74 GB the swap file alone is no better than normal swap, and the compressed modes only help as far as their RAM budget reaches.

Appendix A: how zram and zswap work

Both keep swapped-out memory compressed in RAM. They differ in where they sit in the swap path and in what happens when the compressed memory fills up.

zram: a compressed RAM disk used as a swap device

InnoDB page (anonymous memory)→ swap-out →/dev/zram0 (compressed RAM)→ full →next swap device by priority, or OOM
  • A block device whose storage is compressed RAM; you mkswap / swapon it like a disk.
  • No disk behind it. disksize is the uncompressed capacity; RAM is used only as pages arrive (compressed size + overhead).
  • The kernel picks swap devices by priority and free slots, not by RAM: zram falls back to a lower-priority swap device only when its disksize is full. So disksize is zram's memory budget (≈ disksize ÷ compression ratio). Without a fallback, a full device or exhausted RAM means the OOM killer.
  • Pages never move from zram to the fallback on their own; optional writeback (backing_dev, idle, writeback) moves idle pages when triggered.
  • Incompressible pages are stored as they are and still use RAM.

zswap: a compressed cache in front of disk swap

InnoDB page→ swap-out →zswap pool (compressed RAM)→ pool full / cold / incompressible →swap file on disk
  • Not a device: it intercepts pages on their way to a real swap device and keeps them compressed in RAM. It needs that swap device.
  • The pool is capped at max_pool_percent of RAM. Pages that do not compress well enough (accept_threshold_percent) go straight to disk.
  • Older pages are written back, decompressed, to the swap device when the pool fills, and proactively by the zswap shrinker (on by default in kernel 6.8). A miss is a disk read.
  • Degrades gradually instead of failing: worst case it behaves like disk swap.

Swap modes

# disk: the host's own 8 GB swap file on the datadir SSD
swapon /swapfile

# disk2: swap file on the second SSD, no compression
fallocate -l 64G /data/zstudy-swap.img; chmod 600 /data/zstudy-swap.img; mkswap /data/zstudy-swap.img
swapon /data/zstudy-swap.img

# zswap (lz4 or zstd) in front of the same swap file
echo lz4      > /sys/module/zswap/parameters/compressor        # or zstd
echo zsmalloc > /sys/module/zswap/parameters/zpool
echo 50       > /sys/module/zswap/parameters/max_pool_percent  # 80 and 12 in follow-ups, 32 in round 5
echo Y        > /sys/module/zswap/parameters/enabled
swapon /data/zstudy-swap.img
# defaults kept: accept_threshold_percent 90, shrinker_enabled Y, same_filled_pages_enabled Y

# zram (lz4 or zstd), no disk swap, zswap off
echo N > /sys/module/zswap/parameters/enabled
modprobe zram num_devices=1
echo lz4 > /sys/block/zram0/comp_algorithm   # or zstd
echo 64G > /sys/block/zram0/disksize         # 32G in round 1, 128G in round 5
mkswap /dev/zram0; swapon -p 100 /dev/zram0

# zram + lower-priority disk fallback
echo 12G > /sys/block/zram0/disksize         # 32G in round 5 (~10 GB RAM at ~3.3x)
mkswap /dev/zram0; swapon -p 100 /dev/zram0
swapon -p 10 /data/zstudy-swap.img

# zram writeback: a backing device set before disksize, idle pages written out every minute
losetup -f --show --direct-io=on /data/zstudy-zram-wb.img      # -> /dev/loopN
